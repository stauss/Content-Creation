Context & Stakes
01

A league with a name, a brand, and an ESPN date.

What they did not have was a digital presence.

Major League Pickleball was a new professional sports league launching into a sport that was growing faster than anyone expected. They had a name, a brand identity from an agency, and an ESPN broadcast date. What they did not have was a digital presence — not a site architecture, not a content model, not a system for managing anything that was about to go live in front of a national audience.

The brief was simple on the surface: build a website. The reality was something else entirely. A professional sports league needs to publish real-time scores. It needs a public draft that fans can watch update live. It needs player profiles that connect to teams, teams that connect to events, events that connect to standings — all of it updating constantly during a live broadcast.

Failure here wasn't an abstract risk. It was going to happen on ESPN.

The Problem
02

The Fire Drill

From the outside, it worked. From the inside, it was a controlled emergency.

The first version shipped. The ESPN broadcast happened. From the outside, it worked.

From the inside, it was a controlled emergency. The site had been designed by the branding agency as a static presence — essentially a brochure. When the league went live, the client needed to update scores, publish player information, adjust rosters, and respond to real-time events. None of that had been designed for. Changes were being made directly in the CMS by multiple people simultaneously, with no version control, no rollback, and no structure holding any of it together.

I stayed up almost three days straight supporting the team. Multiple people in the CMS, no version control, everything being fixed on the fly. It was one of the worst working experiences I've had — and it was entirely predictable.

Anything that broke got fixed manually, immediately, regardless of the hour. The system didn't fail because the team wasn't capable. It failed because no one had designed it to do what the sport actually required.

The Decision
03

The Stop

Stop building, and tell the client why.

After the initial launch, the client started doing what clients always do after a successful launch — they started asking for more. New features, new pages, new content types. All reasonable requests. All things that, added on top of what already existed, would eventually collapse the system entirely.

The decision that defined the rest of the project wasn't a design decision. It was a judgment call: stop building, and tell the client why.

If we kept adding to what we had, it was going to fall over fast — and take everyone with it. We needed to stop, understand what the system actually had to do, and build it correctly.

This required a different kind of conversation. Not "what do you want the site to do" but "what does this sport require at a systems level." The league had teams, players, events, scores, standings, a draft, media, and sponsors — all of which needed to relate to each other in ways that would allow anyone to update a single value and have the right things propagate automatically.

The goal wasn't to deliver features. It was to design something that wouldn't break the second a match went live.

The Architecture
04

System Complexity

The core design challenge wasn't visual. It was relational.

A piece of content in a live sports context isn't static — it belongs to multiple contexts simultaneously. A photo taken at an event features specific players who belong to specific teams who competed in that event. That photo should appear on the event page, the player pages, the team pages, and in the media gallery — with all of the relevant metadata intact.

Design the content model wrong and you create a maintenance nightmare: four places to update every time anything changes. Design it right and you update one record, and everything else resolves.

The same logic applied to scoring. A player scores a point. That point belongs to a match. The match belongs to an event. The event feeds into team standings. The team standings update the league table. Get the relationships right and a single score update cascades correctly through the entire system. Get them wrong and you're manually updating six things in real time during a live broadcast.

Update one record. Everything else resolves.

The Approach
05

Design Strategy

Schema first, screens second.

Before any UI work happened on the rebuild, the data model had to be resolved. Every collection was defined: what it contained, what it referenced, what it could not exist without. Only after those relationships were stable did the visual design begin.

The decisions that mattered most during this phase weren't visual — they were about what should be a standalone object and what should be inherited. Teams are standalone objects. Player stats are derived from match records, not stored independently. Team standings are calculated from player performance, not manually entered. These distinctions determined whether the system could support a live event or whether it would require constant manual intervention.

The admin layer was built in Airtable and connected to the CMS for a specific reason: the people running the league during live events were not developers. The content management system had to be simple enough for non-technical staff to update a score mid-match without breaking anything downstream. That constraint shaped every structural decision.

The Result
06

Outcome

Three seasons. No structural failure.

The rebuilt system ran for three seasons without structural failure. A single score update during a live event cascaded correctly through player standings, team standings, and the live bracket — exactly as designed. The public draft aired in real time, with fans watching player picks update live as the broadcast happened.

The client trusted the system enough to bring it into a second league (APP), which launched shortly after. That's not a feature request — it's a confidence signal. They had seen what happens when digital infrastructure is built to match the demands of a live sport, and they wanted it again.

That's not a feature request — it's a confidence signal.

Reflection
07

Reflection

The hardest problems weren't design problems.

The fire drill version was always going to happen. A new league with no digital history, a hard broadcast deadline, and a team that hadn't shipped anything like this before — that first build was inevitable. The real question was whether the lesson from it changed anything.

What I'd do differently: make the architecture conversation happen before any design work, even if that means pushing back on a deadline. The three sleepless days weren't a cost of the project — they were the cost of skipping the hard conversation about what the system actually needed to do. That conversation is now the first thing I have on any project that involves live data, multi-role content management, or relational content.

The other thing worth naming: the hardest problems on this project weren't design problems. They were information architecture and systems problems that happened to have design implications. Recognizing that distinction early — and being willing to work in that space rather than defaulting to screens — is what allowed the system to hold.
