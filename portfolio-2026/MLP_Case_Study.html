Major League Pickleball

From fault to rally.

Three seasons of live sports infrastructure, built around the systems a professional league needs to run live.

Situation
01

A Sport Moving at Speed

Designing for the velocity of a league in motion.

Major League Pickleball was entering the spotlight with a locked ESPN broadcast date, but its digital foundation remained static. In a professional league, a score is not content. It is infrastructure. To survive the pressure of live competition, we had to move beyond the brochure site and build an operating model capable of synchronizing a sport in motion.

The Problem
02

Beyond the Third Shot Drop

When aesthetics meet their breaking point.

The initial launch was a successful illusion. Externally, the design held; internally, it was a manual coordination problem barely surviving a national broadcast. We were treating scores as static content rather than data primitives with cascading consequences. This created a systemic risk: in a live professional league, one broken data relationship doesn't just look bad. It breaks the product for the fans, the media, and the broadcast booth.

The Decision
03

Time for a Timeout

Pausing production to map the relational model behind the sport.

The most critical decision was to stop building features. Before touching a single layout, I deconstructed the sport into a series of cascading relationships that had to hold under live conditions. This relational model became the project's true foundation, ensuring every downstream surface was dependable. It transformed a score from a simple field into a record with consequences, proving that when you engineer the schema first, the screens will always be a true reflection of the sport.

The Architecture
04

Calm in the Kitchen

Designing the invisible logic of the game.

The Stop was an investment in architecture. We replaced manual coordination with a relational engine where data primitives, not design, governed the system. Players connect to Teams; Teams connect to Scores; Scores cascade to Standings. When these relationships are engineered correctly, a single update during a live match propagates across the entire ecosystem automatically, eliminating the broadcast-hour scramble.

This logic extended beyond data to content. A single photo, tagged once, resolves across player profiles, team pages, and event galleries with all metadata intact. By treating the schema as the primary product and the screens as its surface, we ensured the platform remained a true, effortless reflection of the sport.

The schema was the real product. The screens were its surface.

The Interface
05

Designed for the Sideline, Not the Studio

Designing for the heat of the moment.

In live sports, the admin UI is a pressure-management instrument. The people running league operations weren't developers; they were staff updating scores and rosters in real-time while a national audience watched. I designed the internal system to prioritize legibility and obvious actions, ensuring the machine never competed with the match. In high-stakes environments, the admin experience is the product's true fail-safe.

The Proof
06

On the Clock

The ultimate stress test: No room for error.

A live draft and live scoring share the same constraint: both are synchronous, public, and irreversible. When a match is on national television, a score that doesn't update isn't a minor delay. It's a failure in front of everyone watching.

The Season 2 draft required no new infrastructure because the architecture had already solved the hard problem. Players were engineered as relational records, not static content, so moving a name from the draft pool to a roster was a single update. That change resolved across the entire league ecosystem automatically, in real time, in front of a national audience.

We didn't build a draft tool. We built a system that was ready for the draft from day one.

At Scale
07

Performance at Every Altitude

From a deadline emergency to a league standard.

The architecture supported three seasons without structural failure, managing everything from real-time draft events to live scoring. By solving for every altitude, from the scale of the league to the precision of the pixel, we created a logic so resilient it became the architectural standard for the entire ecosystem. The result was a digital foundation the organization could trust.

The influence didn't stop at the league boundary. The UI patterns and structural logic developed for MLP became the de facto visual language for professional pickleball on the web, adopted independently across competing platforms as the sport scaled. When other organizations building in the same space arrive at your design decisions without being asked, you haven't just solved a client's problem. You've set the standard for a category.

When the broadcast is live, the architecture is the experience.

Reflection
08

After the Whistle

The high price of an easy start.

The first season's fire drill was the inevitable cost of moving fast without a foundation. It also made one thing permanent: the architecture conversation, not the feature list, not the design backlog, now happens before a single layout is touched.

The hardest problems on this project were never purely design problems. They were information architecture challenges that happened to have design surfaces. Recognizing that distinction and being willing to work in that space rather than defaulting to screens is what allowed the system to hold for three seasons and keep holding well beyond the initial build.

The scaffolding didn't retire when the engagement did. The same architecture and data model now powers pickleballtournaments.com. The DUPR team leveraged the architecture as a foundation, adapting rather than rebuilding, because the structural decisions had already solved the hard problems. When an external team can skip the blank-page phase entirely because your work already solved it, the design has done more than its job. It has become infrastructure.
